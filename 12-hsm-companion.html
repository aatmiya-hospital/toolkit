The Core HSM Mantra
The foundational affirmation of Himalayan Samarpan Meditation
मैं एक पवित्र आत्मा हूँ
मैं एक शुद्ध आत्मा हूँ
"I am a holy soul · I am a pure soul"
હું એક પવિત્ર આત્મા છું · હું એક શુદ્ધ આત્મા છું
This is the seed-affirmation of HSM. In deep meditation, repeating this mantra — silently, with full attention — gradually dissolves the false identification with the body, thoughts, and ego, allowing awareness to rest in its own pure nature. The mantra is not a request or prayer to something outside — it is a recognition of what was always true.
How to use the mantra in practice
Sit comfortably with spine upright. Close your eyes. Take three slow, deep breaths to settle. Then begin repeating the mantra silently in your mind — "Main ek pavitra aatma hoon, main ek shuddh aatma hoon" — with the pace of your natural breath. When the mind wanders (it will), gently return to the mantra without frustration. No special posture, no special environment, no prior experience is needed.
The meaning — layer by layer
Main (मैं) — The "I" referred to is not the ego-self or the body-mind — it is the witness-awareness that observes all experiences.

Pavitra aatma (पवित्र आत्मा) — Holy soul. Sacred in its very nature — not requiring any ritual, achievement, or condition to be worthy. The word pavitra carries a devotional and spiritual weight in Hindi — it is used for sacred rivers, temples, and scriptures.

Shuddh aatma (शुद्ध आत्मा) — Pure soul. Not corrupted or contaminated by actions, memories, or emotions. Where pavitra points to the sacred, shuddh points to the uncontaminated — the soul as it is beneath all acquired layers.

Repeating both together is a progressive deepening: first the sacredness (pavitra), then the purity (shuddh) — moving from the psychological to the spiritual dimension.
Clinical relevance
For patients with depression, shame-based disorders, OCD (especially religious obsessions), PTSD, and addictions — the core wound is often a deep belief of being contaminated, broken, or fundamentally unworthy. The HSM mantra directly addresses this at a felt, experiential level — not as a cognitive argument, but as a repeated somatic and spiritual affirmation. Sahaj Pranayama — Natural breath awareness
5–10 min
›
Sahaj means "natural" or "effortless." This is not breath control — it is breath observation. The practice develops the witness-awareness that underlies all HSM.
1
Sit comfortably — chair or floor. Spine upright but not rigid. Hands resting gently in lap. Close your eyes softly.
2
Begin to notice the breath. Do not change it — simply observe. Notice the sensation of air entering at the nostrils, the gentle rise of the chest or abdomen, the pause at the top.
3
Observe the outbreath — the quiet release, the natural pause at the bottom. The breath knows what it is doing. You are only watching.
4
When the mind wanders (it will — this is not a failure, it is the nature of mind), gently, without judgment, bring attention back to the breath. Each return is the practice.
5
Gradually introduce the mantra: inhale — "Main ek pavitra aatma hoon", exhale — "Main ek shuddh aatma hoon." Let the mantra ride the breath naturally.
6
Continue for 5–10 minutes. At the end, sit quietly for 1–2 minutes before opening your eyes. Do not rush to re-engage with the world.
Clinical note
Sahaj Pranayama activates the parasympathetic nervous system. In anxious or traumatised patients, it is the safest entry point — breath observation without control avoids the paradoxical hyperventilation that breath-control exercises can trigger in panic disorder.

So-Hum mantra meditation
10–30 min
›
So-Hum (सो-हम्) means "I am That" — a classical Sanskrit mantra that identifies the individual self with universal consciousness. In HSM, So-Hum is used as a vehicle to deepen into the direct experience of the core mantra.
1
Begin with 5 minutes of Sahaj Pranayama to settle the mind and body.
2
With the inhale, silently hear "So" — as if the breath itself is speaking it. With the exhale, silently hear "Hum". Not forced — the mantra simply accompanies the natural breath.
3
After 5–10 minutes of So-Hum, gently transition to the HSM mantra: "Main ek pavitra aatma hoon… main ek shuddh aatma hoon."
4
As the session deepens, the mantra may become more subtle — more felt than thought. Allow this. The mantra is a vehicle, not a destination.
5
Spend the final 5 minutes in silence — simply resting as awareness, without any mantra or technique.

Pratyahara — Sense withdrawal
5–15 min
›
Pratyahara is the 5th limb of Patanjali's Ashtanga Yoga — the voluntary withdrawal of the senses inward. In HSM, it is practised as a transition between external life and deep meditation.
1
Sit still. Begin to notice sound — the farthest sound you can hear, then progressively closer sounds, until you reach the sounds within your own body (heartbeat, breath).
2
With each exhale, imagine the senses withdrawing inward — like a tortoise pulling its limbs inside its shell. Not forcing — allowing.
3
Notice thoughts arising — don't fight them. Simply observe them as objects in awareness, the way you observed sounds. You are the space in which thoughts appear, not the thoughts themselves.
4
Introduce the mantra: "Main ek pavitra aatma hoon." Let it be a returning home whenever you get lost in thoughts or sensations.

Metta Bhavana — Loving-kindness
10–20 min
›
Metta (compassion and loving-kindness) is cultivated in HSM as the natural expression of recognising the purity of all souls — flowing from the core mantra into relationship with others.
1
Begin with the mantra for yourself: "I am a holy soul. I am a pure soul." Feel this as genuinely as you can — not as a wish but as a recognition.
2
Bring to mind someone you love easily — a child, a parent, a dear friend. Silently: "You are a holy soul. You are a pure soul." Wish them well: "May you be peaceful. May you be free from suffering."
3
Expand to a neutral person — someone you neither like nor dislike. Extend the same recognition and wish.
4
Bring to mind a difficult person — someone with whom there is conflict or resentment. This is the practice: "You too are a holy soul." Even if this feels forced at first, the practice gradually dissolves grievance.
5
Finally, expand to all beings: "All souls are holy. All souls are pure." Rest in the sense of universal kinship.
Clinical note
Metta Bhavana has robust clinical evidence for reducing depression, anger, and loneliness. In the HSM framework, it is grounded in the metaphysical — the recognition of soul-purity — rather than merely as a cognitive reframe. This makes it particularly relevant for Indian patients for whom spiritual identity is salient.

About Himalayan Samarpan Meditation
Lineage, founder, and essence
Founder — Param Pujya Shivkrupanand Swami
Himalayan Samarpan Meditation was founded and systematised by Param Pujya Shivkrupanand Swamiji, a spiritual teacher who received the tradition in the Himalayan lineage. Swamiji has dedicated his life to making authentic Himalayan meditation accessible to ordinary people — householders, professionals, students — without requiring renunciation of worldly life.

Swamiji emphasises that the purpose of meditation is not spiritual achievement or special experiences, but samarpan — surrender. The surrender of the ego's false identification, and the recognition of the self's inherent purity and divinity.
Core philosophy — Samarpan
Samarpan (समर्पण) means complete surrender — not passivity, but the active relinquishment of the ego's claim to control. HSM teaches that the root of all suffering is the false identification with the body-mind, and that the direct experience of one's nature as pure, holy awareness dissolves this root.

The practice is simple by design — no complex techniques, no elaborate rituals, no prerequisite spiritual conditions. Anyone who sincerely sits, closes their eyes, and repeats the mantra with attention is doing HSM correctly.
HSM and mental health
HSM is particularly well-suited to a clinical setting because its foundational mantra — "I am a pure soul, I am a holy soul" — directly counters the cognitive-spiritual core of many psychiatric disorders: shame, unworthiness, self-contamination, and hopelessness. Patients with depression, OCD, addiction, and trauma all share a felt sense of being fundamentally broken or impure. The HSM mantra, practised with sincerity over time, gradually replaces this felt sense with one of inherent dignity.

Dr. Dhaivat Mehta is a resource person for HSM activities and integrates HSM-based practices within clinical psychotherapy at Aatmiya Hospital.
Gurutattva — the living presence
HSM teaches the concept of Gurutattva — the principle or essence of the Guru, understood not as a personality but as the living transmission of awakened awareness. For HSM practitioners, Gurutattva is the inner guide that guides the meditation from within, beyond any external dependency. The Gurutattva website (see Links tab) carries Swamiji's teachings, satsangs, and guided sessions.
